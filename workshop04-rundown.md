Welcome! Tonight is about games that *mean something*: how they work, why they stay with us, and how to make one.

You don't need to code. You don't even need to leave with a finished game. Some of us will spend the evening playing and talking about games, some will sketch a game on paper, and some will build one with an AI coding agent. A good conversation, a paper prototype and a shared playable prototype are all good ways to spend the evening.

**The one big idea:**

> **The mechanic is the message.** A game's real story isn't the words on screen — it's the *rules*. Change the rule, change the meaning.

---

## The Framework

Work forwards from what you want a player to feel. Five steps:

> **Intention → Player Experience → Action → System Response → Consequence**

Here's how it reads for **Boardroom** ([play ▶](https://corporate-reign.vercel.app)):

- **Intention:** show that leadership is an impossible balancing act.
- **Player experience:** constant, uncomfortable trade-offs.
- **Action:** swipe left or right on a decision.
- **System response:** four meters move, some up and some down.
- **Consequence:** push any meter too far and your empire collapses, so every later choice gets harder.

Three things to keep in mind tonight:

- **You design, AI assists.** The ideas and decisions are yours. AI helps you explore and build.
- **A working game isn't automatically a meaningful one.** Ask whether it says what you meant.
- **Playtesting is part of making.** Watching someone else play is how you find out what your game actually says.

## The Mini Game Spec

One small template for every mode. Use it on paper, in conversation, or as the start of a prompt for an AI coding agent.

1. **Intention:** What should the player feel, notice, question or understand?
   ____________________________________________
2. **Player action:** What does the player do?
   ____________________________________________
3. **System response:** What happens because of that action?
   ____________________________________________
4. **Consequence:** How does that response change what the player does or experiences next?
   ____________________________________________
5. **Player flow:** How does it begin, develop and end?
   ____________________________________________
6. **Scope:** What's the smallest version worth testing?
   ____________________________________________

Stuck on the intention? The **[worksheet](worksheet.md)** helps you find your one sentence: *"My game is about ____, and I want the player to feel ____."* For a longer version of these questions, see stage 3 of the **[workflow](workflow.md)**.

### Mode A — Play & Discuss

*For storytellers, artists, educators and anyone interested in critical play and game design.*

| Time | What we're doing |
|---|---|
| 18:40 | Deep discussion of the games we played (25 mins) |
| 19:05 | Remix a meaningful mechanic in pairs (25 mins) |
| 19:30 | Paper prototyping & playful testing (25 mins) |
| 19:55 | Exchange ideas & iterate (15 mins) |

**Deep discussion.** Go back to one or two games:

- Where did the game make you complicit, or make you choose?
- What would change if one rule were different?
- What does the game leave out, and why might that matter?

**Pair remix.** Pick a game, from tonight or the **[catalogue](02-pick-a-game.md)**. Change **one rule**, then finish this sentence:

> **"Now the player ________, and the game responds by ________, so it's really about ________."**

More ideas for this in **[Remix](04-remix.md)**.

**Paper prototype.** Turn your remix (or a new idea) into something playable on the table, using cards, dice, tokens, sticky notes or a sketch. One person plays; another acts as "the computer" and applies the rules. Keep it small: one action, one response, one consequence.

**Exchange & iterate.** Swap with another pair. Play their prototype without being told what it's about. Then tell them what you experienced, and let them change one rule.

**Outcome:** A game concept, rule remix or paper prototype.

### Mode B — Co-create Together

*For a small group that wants to design and build one game together.*

| Time | What we're doing |
|---|---|
| 18:40 | Choose a shared idea & intention (15 mins) |
| 18:55 | Define the mechanics & Mini Game Spec (20 mins) |
| 19:15 | Build together with an AI coding agent (35 mins) |
| 19:50 | Playtest, discuss & revise (20 mins) |

**A real example: Window Seat.** At a previous workshop, three of us made **[Window Seat](https://github.com/PlayableStories/window-seat)** ([play ▶](https://playablestories.github.io/window-seat/)) in one evening. Before we start, look at how the idea became a buildable spec:

| Spec | Window Seat |
|---|---|
| **Intention** | *Play it first and say what you think it's about. Then we'll compare.* |
| **Player action** | Raise the window shade. Click clouds, and catch the ones with something faint inside. |
| **System response** | The object (tea, a plant, soup, a painting, a chair) grows and floats into a room taking shape in the sky. Miss it, and it comes back a little clearer. |
| **Consequence** | The room fills, piece by piece. Once the chair arrives, a reflection of the passenger comes to sit at the table. |
| **Player flow** | Shade up → clouds drift → catch five objects → someone familiar sits down → "Cabin crew, prepare for landing" → shade down. |
| **Scope** | One HTML file, no libraries, playable within 15 minutes. |

Now read the **[original prompt](https://github.com/PlayableStories/window-seat/blob/main/Original-Prompt.md)** it was built from. The creative idea is all there, written plainly enough for an AI coding agent to build: the scene, the states in order, what each click does, and how it ends.

**How we'll work:**

1. **Choose an idea together.** Start from something someone said at 18:00. Agree on the intention in one sentence.
2. **Fill in the Mini Game Spec as a group.** Argue about the mechanic, because that's where the meaning is decided.
3. **Build.** One person types the prompt, and the whole group decides what goes in it. Take turns typing, and play every version before asking for changes.
4. **Playtest and revise.** Does it feel the way we intended? Change one thing at a time.

Useful roles to share out:

- **Intention keeper:** asks "does this still say what we meant?"
- **Playtester:** plays each version fresh and says what they notice.
- **Prompter:** types the group's decisions. Rotate this.

→ More on the build steps in **[Realise](06-realise.md)** and the **[workflow](workflow.md)**

**Outcome:** One collaborative playable prototype, where feasible. A clear spec and a good argument about the mechanic are a success too.

### Mode C — Build & Experiment

*For anyone who wants hands-on experience of AI-assisted game making.*

| Time | What we're doing |
|---|---|
| 18:40 | Choose an idea & write a Mini Game Spec (15 mins) |
| 18:55 | Live AI coding demonstration (10 mins) |
| 19:05 | Build, on your own or in a pair (45 mins) |
| 19:50 | Peer playtest & revise (20 mins) |

**Pick your rung:**

1. **Reskin:** keep the rules, change the theme, writing or look. Most people start here, and it's plenty. → **[Reskin](03-reskin.md)**
2. **Remix:** change a rule to create a different meaning. → **[Remix](04-remix.md)**
3. **Realise:** design and build a new game from scratch. → **[Realise](06-realise.md)**

Starting from a catalogue game? Open the **[catalogue](02-pick-a-game.md)**, import it into Replit, press **Run**, and play it once as-is.

**Live demo.** We'll take one Mini Game Spec and build it live, so you can see the loop before you try it.

**The build loop, whatever you're building:**

1. Describe the game in plain English: your intention and your mechanic.
2. Run it and play it before you ask for anything else.
3. Change one thing at a time. If the AI changes too much: *"Undo that last change — I preferred it before."*

→ **[Prompt library](prompts/)** · **[Fixing things](prompts/fixing-things.md)**

**Peer playtest.** Hand your game to someone without explaining it. Watch them play. Ask what they felt, then revise.

**Outcome:** Individual or small-team playable prototypes.

> 💡 Your first attempt might miss. **That's the workshop working, not failing.** Change the rule and try again.

## Facilitator Notes

*For the people running the session. Attendees can skip this.*

**Choosing a mode**

- Decide from what people **say they want** at 18:00–18:40, not from headcount or job titles. A developer may want to talk; an artist may want to build.
- Listen to the 18:00 question (talk / make together / build) and to who leans in during the 18:25 discussion.
- **Mixed interests:** split into small groups running different modes, as long as there's a facilitator for each group that's building.
- **Very small groups:** co-creation (Mode B) works well, as it did with Window Seat, but offer it rather than forcing it.
- You can switch modes mid-session if the energy moves. Say so out loud.

**Running co-creation**

- Participants make the creative decisions: the intention, the mechanic, and what to change after each playtest. If they're just watching you prompt the AI, stop and hand the decision back.
- Hand over the keyboard. Rotate the prompter role.

**Protect the time**

- Playtesting and the 20:10 showcase aren't optional. If building overruns, cut build time, not reflection.
- In every mode, bring people back to "what did you intend, and what happened?"

**Fallbacks**

- Keep a **tested game** ready to play, for example Window Seat open in a tab, in case building stalls.
- Keep a **paper activity** ready (Mode A's pair remix and paper prototype) in case the WiFi or AI tools fail.
