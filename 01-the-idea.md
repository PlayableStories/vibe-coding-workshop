# The Idea: Games That Mean Something

Let's slow down for a moment, before any building, and talk about what we're actually trying to make. It's worth it — the building is the easy part.

## Games already tell stories. Just not the way you think.

When we say a game "has a story," we usually picture words: an opening crawl, characters talking, an ending screen. Cutscenes.

But that's the story *bolted on top*. The story a player actually *lives* is in the rules — in what the game makes them do, again and again, and how it makes them feel about it.

Think about it:

- A game where you can only move forward, never back, *feels* like regret.
- A game where helping someone costs you points *feels* like sacrifice.
- A game where you can never quite catch up *feels* like the treadmill of modern work.

Nobody had to write "this is about regret" on the screen. The rule said it. That's what we mean by:

> **The mechanic is the message.**

## So what's a "playable story"?

A small game where the *rules* carry the meaning. You don't read the message — you play it, and you feel it.

Our sample games each do this with exactly one clear idea:

- **Pill Match** — a matching game where matching *wrong* hurts you. → *Taking medicine safely is about restraint, not greed.*
- **Memory of Home** — cards match on object *or* colour, never both. → *A memory can be true without being whole.*
- **Half of Me** — every match gives you only half a memory. → *When you lose your home, you keep the place or the feeling — not both.*
- **Boardroom** — every decision pleases one group and angers another. → *Leadership is an impossible balancing act.*
- **Office Chair Racing** — every promotion just brings faster, scarier competition. → *Climbing the ladder doesn't make you safe.*

Notice how each one fits in a single sentence. That's the goal. If you can't say what your game is about in one sentence, it isn't ready yet — and that's what the **[worksheet](worksheet.md)** is for.

## The ladder you'll climb

You don't build from scratch. You stand on a game that already works and change it. There are three rungs:

### Rung 1 — Reskin
Keep the rules. Change the *words* and the *look*. Boardroom is about a CEO; you could make it about a head teacher, a hospital ward, a parent. The dilemmas, the art, the tone — all yours. **Most people start here, and it's plenty.**

### Rung 2 — Remix
Change *one rule*, so the meaning shifts. This is the magic rung. Half of Me was made by taking Memory of Home and changing how matching works. One rule. A whole new feeling. We'll do this together.

### Rung 3 — Rebuild
Describe a brand-new game in plain language and let the AI build it from nothing. Ambitious, exciting, and a great thing to try once you've got the hang of the first two.

## Why this works without coding

Because the AI writes the code. You direct it.

A non-coder's real skill here isn't typing — it's *knowing what to ask for*, and *recognising when you've got it.* That's design. That's taste. That's the thing you already have and the machine doesn't.

The sentences you say to the AI are your real material. We've gathered the most useful ones in the **[prompt library](prompts/)** so you're never staring at a blank box wondering how to phrase it.
